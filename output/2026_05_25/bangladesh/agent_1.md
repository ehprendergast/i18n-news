Bangladesh operates as a parliamentary democracy with a unicameral legislature, though the country faces ongoing infrastructure challenges in its governmental systems [2]. The nation's political structure oversees a territory of approximately 148,460 square kilometers, bordered primarily by India along 4,096 kilometers and Myanmar along 27 kilometers to the southeast [1]. The capital city of Dhaka sits fewer than 25 feet above sea level, reflecting the low-lying deltaic geography that characterizes much of the country's administrative landscape [2].

The Bangladeshi economy has sustained nearly six percent annual growth over two decades, with visible development particularly evident in Dhaka [2]. This economic trajectory has positioned the country to meet several Millennium Development Goals according to a 2011 United Nations Development Program Progress Report [2]. Despite this growth, Bangladesh remains classified as a developing country with significant infrastructure deficiencies [2]. The economy's foundation rests heavily on agriculture, supported by the fertile alluvial soil of the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta, which comprises the largest portion of the country's geography [1].

The country's social structure remains male-dominated, with social classes playing an integral though diminishing role in society [2]. Traditional family structures persist, with few nursing homes and no government-provided retirement programs, leading grandparents to frequently assist with childcare while parents work [2]. Marriage customs often involve arrangements through intermediaries called "ghatak," with men typically marrying after completing education or achieving financial security [2].

Bangladeshi cultural life centers around artistic traditions that form a source of national pride [2]. The national sport is Kabaddi, a competitive team game where participants attempt to score points by touching opposing team members [2]. Dietary customs emphasize rice as the primary food, with preferences for spicy preparations often marinated in shu'ra, a mixture of chopped onions and spices [2]. Social etiquette includes eating with the right hand and washing after meals, with food served individually rather than passed around tables [2].

# Japan: International Briefing

*Report date: December 19, 2024*

Japan's parliamentary system operates through a bicameral Diet comprising 465 members in the House of Representatives and 245 members in the House of Councillors [4]. The administrative structure encompasses the Prime Minister's Office, Cabinet Secretariat, and 14 ministries and agencies [4]. The judicial framework functions through a hierarchical court system including the Supreme Court, High Courts, District Courts, Summary Courts, and Family Courts [4]. National symbols include the Nisshoki flag, known as Hinomaru, and the national anthem Kimi ga Yo [4].

Japan maintains its position as the world's third-largest economy by nominal GDP, recording US$4.975 trillion as of 2020 [4]. GDP per capita stands at US$34,366, reflecting the country's advanced economic status [4]. Primary industries centre on automobiles, consumer electronics, computers, and other electronics manufacturing [4]. The tourism sector attracts tens of millions of visitors annually, contributing to economic activity across the archipelago's nine distinct tourism regions [6].

The country's population totals 125,284,630 as of June 2021, distributed across 377,975.26 square kilometres spanning 47 prefectures [4]. Tokyo Metropolitan Area houses 13,999,568 residents, representing approximately 10 percent of the national population [4]. Religious practices encompass Shintoism, Buddhism, and Christianity [4]. The nation's geographic diversity extends from Hokkaido in the north to Okinawa in the south, creating distinct regional cultural identities [2].

Seasonal tourism patterns reflect Japan's climatic variations throughout the year. Cherry blossom season occurs from late March through April, with peak blooming in early April across major cities including Tokyo, Osaka, and Kyoto [5][6]. Spring temperatures range from 5°C to 20°C, attracting visitors to locations such as Tokyo's Ueno Park, Meguro River, and Hirosaki Castle in Aomori [5][6]. The Takayama Festival occurs during this period, featuring traditional performances [5].

Summer festival season brings major cultural events across multiple regions. Tokyo's Sumida River Fireworks display takes place in late July, whilst Aomori's Nebuta Matsuri occurs in early August featuring giant paper-covered floats [6]. Tokushima's Awa-Odori dance festival follows in mid-August [6]. Autumn season, from late October to November, offers temperatures between 14°C and 25°C for foliage viewing activities, including onsen bathing at Jozankei Onsen and scenic journeys on the Kurobe Gorge Railway in Toyama [5][6].

Winter tourism centres on Hokkaido's Sapporo Snow Festival in early February, featuring elaborate ice sculptures [6]. Cultural attractions span traditional and contemporary offerings, with Tokyo featuring Meiji Shrine, Asakusa's Sensō-ji temple, and modern districts including Shibuya and Harajuku [3]. Kyoto, which served as Japan's capital for over a thousand years, maintains cultural significance through sites including Fushimi Inari Shrine, Kinkaku-ji Golden Pavilion, the Gion District, and Arashiyama Bamboo Grove [3].

Entertainment venues continue expanding their offerings to attract domestic and international visitors. Universal Studios Japan features the 'Universal Summer Matsuri Night Neon Glow Up' beginning in July and the upcoming 'One Piece Premier Summer 2026' attraction [1]. The gaming sector maintains Japan's reputation as a destination "where serious players go to level up," reflecting the country's position in electronic entertainment [1]. Traditional festivals such as the Heike Taisai Grand Festival continue drawing visitors seeking cultural immersion [1].

Tourism sector marketing employs digital engagement strategies, with promotional campaigns encouraging tourists to share travel experiences using hashtag initiatives [1]. Transportation infrastructure has undergone recent adjustments, with East Japan Railway Company implementing revised fares and charges, whilst ANA Group announced modified flight schedules [1]. These developments reflect ongoing adaptations within Japan's tourism and transportation sectors.

The country's tourism infrastructure spans from Hokkaido's natural landscapes to Okinawa's island culture, with each of the nine regions offering specialised attractions ranging from traditional temples to modern entertainment venues [2]. Regional diversity encompasses Tohoku's winter festivals, Kanto's metropolitan attractions, Chubu's mountainous terrain, Kansai's cultural centres, and the distinct offerings of Chugoku, Shikoku, and Kyushu [2].

Japan's positioning in the global economy reflects its manufacturing capabilities and technological development across multiple sectors. The combination of traditional cultural attractions and contemporary entertainment offerings creates a diverse tourism portfolio that operates throughout the year, capitalising on seasonal variations and regional specialities across the archipelago [4][5][6].

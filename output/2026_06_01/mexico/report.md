# Mexico: International Briefing

*Report date: December 19, 2024*

Mexico's federal authorities implement differentiated travel restrictions for government personnel based on regional risk assessments, reflecting the varied security landscape across the country's 1,972,550 square kilometres [2][1]. The state of Colima faces particular security challenges due to violence from criminal organisations, with most homicides constituting targeted assassinations [2]. Emergency services remain limited in remote locations throughout the country, constraining administrative capacity in certain regions [2].

The tourism sector demonstrates mixed performance indicators for 2023. Mexico ranks among the top 10 most favoured tourist destinations globally according to the Secretariat of Tourism, yet dropped five places in global ranking for foreign currency collection to 15th position [5]. Despite this decline in relative standing, the country collected a historic sum of $30,809.5 million in tourist spending during 2023 [5].

International connectivity continues expanding through new aviation routes. Italian airline Neos launched direct flights between Italy and Cancun beginning December 22nd with weekly Sunday flights, enhancing European access to Mexico's Caribbean destinations [5]. This development reflects ongoing infrastructure investment in the tourism sector despite the country's declining position in global currency collection rankings.

Mexico's geographical diversity underpins its tourism appeal, spanning from the Sierra Madre Occidental, which extends from the northern border southeast through various regions, to coastal destinations along both Pacific and Atlantic shores [1]. The country's highest point, Pico de Orizaba Volcano, reaches 5,636 metres, while the Central Mexican Plateau covers much of northern and central Mexico at an average elevation of 1,825 metres above sea level [1]. Copper Canyon stands as North America's deepest canyon, forming part of the Sierra Madre system [1].

Cultural heritage preservation operates through the Pueblos Mágicos programme, which designates towns that preserve historical, cultural and natural heritage [5]. These locations maintain symbolic significance and historical importance while serving as tourism alternatives for national and foreign visitors [5]. The programme reflects institutional efforts to balance heritage conservation with economic development through tourism.

Regional tourism patterns reflect Mexico's geographical divisions. Northern regions feature colonial heritage in cities such as Puebla, Guadalajara, and Guanajuato [5]. Eastern areas include Veracruz and Gulf coast destinations, while western regions encompass Acapulco, Oaxaca, and Tuxtla Gutiérrez [5]. Southern territories contain Riviera Maya and archaeological sites including Palenque in Chiapas [5].

Archaeological tourism centres particularly on the Yucatan Peninsula, which features sites including Chichen Itza, Tulum and Coba [5]. These pre-Columbian sites attract both domestic and international visitors, contributing to the region's tourism economy alongside coastal resort destinations.

Coastal destinations include Cabo San Lucas, Sayulita, and Puerto Escondido, offering water-based activities to visitors [4]. The diversity of coastal environments spans both Pacific and Caribbean shores, supporting varied forms of marine tourism and recreational activities.

Athletic events contribute to domestic tourism through competitions such as the Xtrail Deprivé Challenge, which attracted two thousand runners in its tenth anniversary at Bernal vineyards [5]. Such events combine sporting activities with regional tourism promotion, demonstrating the intersection of recreational and cultural tourism.

Hydrological resources include approximately 150 rivers flowing through Mexico, with 70% draining into the Pacific Ocean [1]. This extensive river system supports both agricultural activities and regional water management requirements across the country's diverse geographical zones.

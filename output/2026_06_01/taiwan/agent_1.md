Taiwan's administrative structure comprises 22 administrative divisions: 13 counties, 3 cities, and 6 special municipalities under the Executive Yuan's jurisdiction [4]. The counties include Changhua, Chiayi, Hsinchu, Hualien, Kinmen, Lienchiang, Miaoli, Nantou, Penghu, Pingtung, Taitung, Yilan, and Yunlin, while the three cities are Chiayi, Hsinchu, and Keelung [4]. The six special municipalities are Kaohsiung, New Taipei, Taichung, Tainan, Taipei, and Taoyuan [4]. Taipei serves as the capital city, located in northern Taiwan [4].

The island's democratization accelerated during the 1980s, culminating in the founding of the Democratic Progressive Party in 1986 and the lifting of martial law in 1987 [6]. Taiwan conducted its first legislative elections in over four decades in 1992, followed by its first direct presidential election in 1996 [6]. The political system has demonstrated stability through peaceful transfers of power, with the KMT losing to the DPP in 2000, followed by additional democratic transitions in 2008 and 2016 [6].

Taiwan's population stands at approximately 23.6 million people across an area of 35,980 square kilometers [6]. The island maintains a population density of 656 people per square kilometer [6]. Taiwan operates under its own constitution with democratically-elected leaders, maintaining separate governance from mainland China [7]. The dominant political issues center on economic reform, growth, and the management of cross-strait relations [6].

The economy has achieved significant development, with gross domestic product reaching $611.39 billion and per capita GDP of $32,300 [6]. The currency in circulation is the New Taiwan dollar [6]. Taiwan emerged as one of East Asia's economic "Tigers" and became a major investor in mainland China after 2000 as cross-strait economic ties expanded [6]. China remains Taiwan's largest trading partner, with many Taiwanese maintaining business connections across the strait [7].

Taiwan's geographic position subjects it to frequent seismic activity, experiencing 15,000 to 18,000 earthquakes annually, with 800 to 1,000 perceptible to residents [4]. The island features mountainous terrain in its eastern two-thirds, with the Chung-yang Shan range dominating the central region and containing over 200 peaks exceeding 3,000 meters in elevation [4]. Yu Shan represents the highest point at 3,952 meters [4]. The western portion consists of rolling hills descending to relatively flat plains [4].

The island contains over 150 rivers and streams originating in the central mountains, with the Choshui and Kaoping among the most significant [4]. Sun Moon Lake constitutes the largest body of water [4]. Taiwan operates nine national parks and features more than 100 hot springs distributed across the island [8]. The coastline extends 1,566.3 kilometers, encompassing both golden and black-sand beaches [4][8].

Cultural practices emphasize the concept of "face," which corresponds to status and respect [5]. Family structures encompass children, parents, extended relatives, elders, and ancestors, with many Taiwanese residing in compact living arrangements [5]. Western-style clothing predominates, with business suits considered appropriate for formal occasions [5]. Dining customs center on food sharing, with dishes typically placed communally for group consumption [5].

Popular recreational activities include golf, ping-pong, basketball, soccer, softball, and badminton [5]. Health clubs and gymnasiums receive regular usage [5]. The island contains over 70 night markets across Taiwan, with more than 30 located in Taipei, New Taipei, and Keelung [8]. Taiwanese cuisine incorporates influences from Chinese, Japanese, and indigenous traditions, with xiao long bao (soup dumplings) representing a signature dish [8]. Tea culture maintains significance in social practices [8].

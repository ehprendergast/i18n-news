# Ireland: International Briefing

*Report date: December 19, 2024*

Ireland operates as a republic in Western Europe, maintaining United Nations membership since 1955, which officials characterise as central to Irish foreign policy [2]. The country participates actively in European Union economic and social policy formation, with government communications positioning Ireland as part of Europe's open community [2]. Ireland's international engagement extends through what officials describe as a natural network of global influence, facilitated by a diaspora numbering over 70 million people worldwide [2].

The Irish government maintains an official international website promoting the country's integration within European frameworks [2]. Government communications emphasise Ireland's quality of life rankings on global indices, alongside commitments to both domestic and international environmental protection [2]. These promotional efforts highlight Ireland's cultural appeal as part of broader international positioning strategies.

Ireland encompasses approximately 70,273 square kilometres in Western Europe, sharing a land border with Northern Ireland to the north [3]. The country comprises four distinct geographical regions: the Central Lowlands, Western Coastal area, Eastern and Southern Coastal area, and Northern Uplands [3]. The Central Lowlands contain numerous lakes and peat bogs, with peatlands constituting roughly 20 percent of the country's total landmass [3].

Climate considerations are affecting traditional peat extraction practices across Ireland's extensive bog systems [3]. The western coastal areas feature notable geographical landmarks including the Cliffs of Moher [3]. Carrauntoohil stands as Ireland's highest point at 1,041 metres, located in the western region [3]. The River Shannon flows as the country's longest river, originating from Cuilcagh Mountain slopes and extending through the Central Lowlands to the Atlantic Ocean [3].

The western areas include Gaeltacht regions where Irish language continues to be spoken, representing ongoing cultural preservation efforts [3]. These linguistic communities persist within Ireland's broader demographic landscape, contributing to the country's cultural diversity that government sources promote internationally.

Mexico's diverse geography encompasses distinct recreational and cultural zones that draw both domestic and international visitors. The country's coastal regions along the Pacific Ocean and Caribbean Sea feature prominent beach destinations including Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Cabo San Lucas, and Puerto Vallarta [4]. The Yucatán Peninsula offers opportunities for swimming with whale sharks and scuba diving along the world's second-largest reef system in Cozumel [4]. On the Pacific coast, Puerto Escondido provides turtle release programmes and bioluminescent plankton tours, while Sayulita serves as a surfing destination near Puerto Vallarta [4]. Football remains the predominant recreational activity, with children playing the sport in schools, streets, and parks throughout the country [2].

The nation's cultural landscape features significant archaeological sites and natural landmarks that define its tourism sector. Chichen Itza, recognised as one of the New Seven Wonders of the World, and Teotihuacan, formerly among the largest Mayan cities, represent major pre-Columbian heritage sites [4]. The Copper Canyon in the Sierra Madre Occidental constitutes the deepest canyon system in North America, with the mountain range's highest point at Cerro Mohinora reaching 3,250 metres [1]. Mexico's volcanic geography includes the Pico de Orizaba, the country's highest peak at 5,636 metres and the third-highest mountain in North America [1]. The government promotes cultural tourism through initiatives including the Pueblos Mágicos programme and maintains a cultural calendar of events [3].

Mexican social structure typically centres on large family units, particularly in rural areas, though urban families tend to be smaller [2]. Traditional dietary staples include corn, beans, rice, and chillies, combined with spices, vegetables, and proteins in daily meals [2]. Social customs emphasise personal relationships over strict scheduling, and the population generally expresses pride in national identity [2]. Urban areas predominantly feature Western-style clothing, while traditional dress persists in certain regions [2]. Hospitality customs include welcoming unannounced visitors and serving refreshments, with dining etiquette requiring both hands to remain above the table [2].

Mexico's economy faces ongoing challenges related to law enforcement capacity and organised crime activity. State and local police forces experience insufficient training and funding, creating enforcement gaps that criminal organisations exploit [2]. Municipal police in some areas face suspicion of collaboration with organised crime, while officers in other regions become targets of criminal groups [2]. The sale of counterfeit and pirated goods represents a significant economic sector largely controlled by organised crime networks, though personal purchase remains legal [2]. These security conditions affect both domestic commerce and international tourism, despite the majority of visitors experiencing no difficulties [2].

The federal government has implemented measures to strengthen national law enforcement capabilities, though regional disparities persist [2]. Mexico's geographical position, bordered by the Pacific Ocean, Gulf of Mexico, and Caribbean Sea, along with land borders with three countries, influences its economic and security dynamics [1]. The Central Mexican Plateau, spanning much of the country's northern and central regions at an average elevation of 1,825 metres, contains significant desert and shrubland areas [1]. Approximately 150 rivers traverse the country, with 70% draining into the Pacific Ocean, though most remain unnavigable [1].

Nigeria occupies a strategic position in West Africa, bordered by the Republic of Benin to the west, Niger to the north, Chad to the northeast, and Cameroon to the east [1][2]. The country extends along 853 kilometers of Atlantic coastline through the Gulf of Guinea, providing significant maritime access [1][2]. With a total area of approximately 923,769 square kilometers, Nigeria sits between 4° and 14° north latitude and 3° and 15° east longitude, positioning it as Africa's 14th largest country by landmass [2].

The nation's geography encompasses distinct regional zones that influence agricultural and economic activity. The northern savanna region features grasslands and scattered vegetation suitable for cattle herding and grain cultivation, transitioning into the semi-arid Sahel zone with sparse rainfall patterns [1]. The central highlands, including the Jos Plateau reaching 1,295 meters in elevation, contain significant mineral resources and support tin mining operations alongside diverse crop production [1].

Nigeria's southern regions demonstrate greater agricultural diversity through the Guinea Savanna belt, where increased rainfall supports cultivation of yam, cassava, and maize [1]. The country's highest peak, Chappal Waddi, reaches 2,419 meters in the eastern section of this zone [1]. Further south, the rainforest belt experiences heavy rainfall and high humidity, though extensive deforestation has occurred due to logging and agricultural conversion [1].

The Niger Delta region along the coast features mangrove forests, swamps, and lagoons where the Niger River system drains into the Atlantic Ocean [1]. This densely populated area serves as the center of Nigeria's oil industry, though petroleum extraction has generated environmental challenges including oil spills and land degradation [1]. The delta's complex ecosystem of waterways and wetlands represents a critical economic zone despite ongoing environmental pressures.

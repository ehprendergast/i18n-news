Colombia: International Briefing

Report date: December 19, 2024

Colombia's political landscape has been marked by significant controversy and electoral competition in recent months. President Gustavo Petro generated widespread criticism by publicly displaying his marked ballot during voting and subsequently stating he does not recognize the results of the presidential election [3]. The first round of the 2026 presidential election revealed tight competition between right-wing candidate Abelardo De la Espriella and left-wing candidate Ivan Cepeda, with polls closing at 4:00 p.m. [3]. The electoral process resulted in Ivan Cepeda, representing the governing Historic Pact party, losing the first-round election [3]. Former conservative presidential candidate Paloma Valencia became the first major traditional right-wing figure to rally behind De la Espriella following the initial results [3].

The country continues to manage long-standing security challenges stemming from low-intensity conflicts involving guerrilla groups, paramilitary militias, drug traffickers, and corruption dating to 1964-1966 [2]. The Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) and the National Liberation Army (ELN) began guerrilla insurgency campaigns around this period, creating ongoing governance complications for successive administrations [2].

Colombia maintains its position as the fourth-largest country in South America after Brazil, Argentina, and Peru, with a population of approximately 53 million people [2][4]. The Colombian peso serves as the official currency, with exchange rate fluctuations affecting international trade dynamics [4]. Arab Gulf States have increased their investments across multiple Colombian sectors, including mining, renewable energy, and agribusiness [3]. The country's unique geographic positioning as the only South American nation with both Atlantic and Pacific coastlines provides strategic advantages for regional trade and logistics operations [2].

Colombia's physical geography demonstrates remarkable diversity, featuring more varied terrain than any other area of comparable size in Latin America [2]. The western Cordillera Occidental mountain range reaches peaks of 13,000 feet, while the Cordillera Central contains snow-clad volcanoes with summits above 18,000 feet [2]. The nation is bordered by Venezuela, Brazil, Ecuador, Peru, Panama, and both the Atlantic and Pacific oceans [2].

Colombian society reflects strong family-centered values, with family unity and support remaining highly valued cultural attributes [1]. Personal appearance tends toward conservative yet fashionable dress, with men in larger cities wearing suits and ties while women opt for comfortable dresses [1]. Social customs include informal visiting patterns where friends and relatives often arrive without prior notice, and people commonly sit on verandas to chat with passersby [1]. Dating typically begins around ages 14 or 15, depending on family customs and regional practices [1].

Regional diversity significantly influences Colombian culture through varied food, music, and dance traditions shaped by different climates and geographic zones [1]. The term 'rumbero' characterizes a notable aspect of Colombian cultural identity [1]. Colombian breakfast foods include juice, coffee, hot chocolate, fruit, eggs, bread, or changua [1]. Popular dating activities among young people include attending movies, restaurants, and discos [1].

Soccer remains the most popular sport in Colombia, with widespread participation among children and significant viewership of professional matches [1]. The country has achieved international recognition in various cultural spheres, with Colombian artist Juan Covelli gaining prominence for visual art addressing social issues, including his recent work 'Atrato' named after a Colombian river [3]. In the culinary sector, artisanal cheesemaker HolaAndes from Guatavita, Cundinamarca, won a gold medal at the World Cheese Awards [3].

Colombian cultural influence extends internationally through various channels. Chef Fernando Arevalo operates Singapore's only Colombian restaurant, Latido, showcasing Colombian cuisine in Southeast Asia [3]. These developments reflect the growing international recognition of Colombian cultural products and artistic expression.
